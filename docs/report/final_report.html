1. Summary

The project builds a scheduler that decides, for each LLM request on a single CPU machine, how the model should be executed. TinyLlama-1.1B runs layer by layer through a hand-written decode loop. The action space is three thread budgets: {3, 6, 10} threads. Layer staging is the execution mechanism used to enforce these budgets across the 22 decoder layers: static executes all 22 layers in a single stage with 10 threads; latency_optimized executes all 22 layers in a single stage with 6 threads; and balanced splits the layers into two sequential stages (layers 0-10 and 11-21) executed one after the other at 3 threads each, running at a 3-thread budget throughout generation. Because execution is single-device CPU inference, stages run sequentially without parallel pipelining. All three strategies yield token-identical output to Hugging Face model.generate(), so the choice only affects latency.

The work went through seven phases. The early versions (v1/v2) produced results that turned out to be noise, because the strategies were computationally identical and were measured one after another. v3 fixed both problems. Strategies now differ in thread budgets {3, 6, 10}, and measurements are paired and randomized under controlled background CPU load. A contextual-bandit DQN was then trained on the v3 data. After that the project moved to its main idea, re-deciding the strategy every 10 tokens during generation, plus an online bandit that keeps learning from measured token latency.

v7 main run (pre-registered, 450 runs; section 16)
  • Against the best fixed arm in each scenario (chosen from the same data; descriptive), the lookup rule built from the v7 load sweep (rule_lookup) was 0.1% to 4.8% faster. The largest gain was in spike, where it was faster in all 10 contexts; in steady_idle it tied (experiments/e4_best_fixed.csv).
  • Pre-registered tests (paired t, Holm over 20): rule_lookup beat fixed threads_2 in all five scenarios (-8% to -20% vs threads_2) and fixed balanced in steady_idle, spike and relief (-13%, -5%, -9% vs balanced). Against balanced in steady_heavy and ramp (-2%), the differences did not survive Holm correction. 15 of 20 tests were significant.
  • Policies built on direct measurements of this machine beat the offline DQN. The online bandit, warm-started from the same sweep, nearly tied rule_lookup (descriptive). The DQN did not generalise across prompts: it picked the best arm in 35% of held-out contexts offline, and online it was 10% to 60% slower than rule_lookup in four of five scenarios. The core-count heuristic was significantly slower in three scenarios.
  • 106 of 450 runs had a paging event. With those removed (n = 3-9 per test) the direction of every primary effect is unchanged, but fewer tests survive Holm correction.
Earlier results (v3-v5)
  • Which strategy is fastest depends on load. Idle machine: 10 threads (static) wins. Around 8 busy workers: 6 threads. At 10-14 busy workers: 3+3 threads (balanced). At 12 workers static oversubscribes the CPU and is about 18x slower per token than balanced (2.14 s vs 0.116 s per token).
  • In all five scenarios, the re-deciding DQN (rl_adaptive) and the 60% threshold rule (rule_adaptive) are statistically indistinguishable (Holm-adjusted p = 1.0, mean differences -0.6 to +12.5 s). The gain comes from intra-generation re-deciding, not from reinforcement learning.
  • Compared to the best fixed baseline, rl_adaptive did not show a statistically significant advantage in any scenario after Holm adjustment across the 30 predefined comparisons. In ramp (all contexts, n=8), rl_adaptive was 16% slower than fixed balanced (68.9 s vs 59.4 s, mean diff +9.52 s, Holm p = 1.0). In spike, adaptive policies lost 21.8 s to fixed balanced (59.5 s vs 37.7 s, Holm p = 0.44) due to reaction delay.
  • For rl_adaptive versus rl_once in ramp, the all-context mean difference was -238.40 s (95% paired bootstrap CI [-366.99, -101.04]; raw paired-t p = 0.0134). After Holm adjustment across the 30 comparisons, Holm p = 0.31, which does not survive the 0.05 family-wise significance threshold.
  • For rl_adaptive, the only comparisons that survived Holm adjustment in the all-context primary analysis were against static in ramp (-353.7 s, Holm p = 0.0009) and spike (-73.7 s, Holm p = 0.0268), reflecting the severe oversubscription cliff of 10 threads under external load. Across all 30 tests in the family, the only other comparison that survived Holm adjustment was rule_adaptive versus balanced in relief (-3.66 s, Holm p = 0.0177).
  • Token IDs remained identical across all seven conditions in all 40 v5 contexts. The source data has no per-segment counterfactual action outcomes, so an offline segment oracle cannot be computed on v5.

2. Problem and goal

LLM inference on a shared machine competes with other processes for CPU. A single fixed thread budget is only best for one load level. Too many threads under contention cause oversubscription and context switching. Too few threads on an idle machine waste cores. The goal is a scheduler that observes the request and the system state and picks the execution plan (thread budgets {3, 6, 10} applied via layer staging) that minimizes latency, without changing the model's output.

Most schedulers decide once per request, before the first token. A long response can take tens of seconds, and load can change during that time, so the per-request decision goes stale. The core method evaluated here is intra-generation re-scheduling. Because every strategy shares the same weights and the same KV cache, the engine can switch thread budgets between tokens without recomputation overhead and with identical output.

4. System design

4.1 Layer-sliced inference engine

src/llm/inference.py runs TinyLlama without model.generate(). It embeds the prompt, then runs the decoder layers stage by stage with a shared KV cache, applies the final norm and LM head, and samples greedily. Layer staging is the execution mechanism: before each stage it sets torch.set_num_threads() to that stage's intra-op budget. Because all stages execute sequentially on the CPU, partitioning serves as an intra-request thread budgeting mechanism rather than parallel pipeline parallelism. Between stages it records an activation handoff (an in-process clone(), timed and sized). The model is loaded lazily and once.

generate_partitioned_response(prompt, strategy, scheduler=..., decision_interval=N) accepts a scheduler object. Every N tokens it calls scheduler.decide() and switches strategy when asked. All runs in v4/v5 generate exactly 200 tokens (ignore_eos), so latencies are comparable across conditions.

4.2 Execution strategies

The action space consists of three thread budgets: {3, 6, 10} threads. Layer staging applies these budgets across the 22 decoder layers:

IDStrategyThread budgetLayer stagesThread allocation per stageExecution behavior
0static100-21 (single stage)10 threadsRuns all 22 layers at 10 threads
1balanced30-10, 11-21 (two stages)3 threads, 3 threadsRuns layers 0-10 at 3 threads, then layers 11-21 at 3 threads sequentially (3 threads continuous)
2latency_optimized60-21 (single stage)6 threadsRuns all 22 layers at 6 threads

In balanced, stage 2 executes only after stage 1 completes. Because the two stages run sequentially one after the other at 3 threads each, in effect balanced runs at 3 threads throughout generation. Strategies are defined in configs/config.yaml and loaded by src/config.py. src/llm/partition.py turns them into stage definitions, and src/graph/execution_graph.py builds a networkx execution graph annotated with stage layer ranges and thread counts.

4.3 Telemetry and load generation

  • ExternalLoadMonitor measures system CPU minus this process's own CPU, so the scheduler reacts to other processes and not to its own inference threads.
  • BackgroundLoadSampler (v4) runs that measurement in a daemon thread with smoothing. Reading it never blocks generation. In v3, blocking telemetry cost 0.21 s per request.
  • BackgroundCPULoad starts N busy-loop worker processes for a whole request. DynamicCPULoad changes N over time. TokenScheduledLoad (v4) changes N after a given number of generated tokens, so every policy meets the load change at exactly the same point of the response.

4.4 Schedulers and policies

ConditionWhat it does
static, balanced, latency_optimizedFixed strategy for the whole request (baselines)
rule_adaptiveThreshold rule on external CPU (60% in v5): below it use static, above it balanced. Re-decided every 10 tokens with hysteresis
rl_onceOffline DQN, decides once before the first token
rl_adaptiveSame DQN, re-decides every 10 tokens with hysteresis
bandit_adaptiveOnline contextual bandit (OnlineBanditPolicy) that learns from measured seconds per token while it serves

Hysteresis (patience=2) means a policy only switches after it proposes the same new strategy in two decisions in a row. This stops flapping on noisy readings, but it also means a switch takes at least 20 tokens after the load changes.

4.5 The online bandit

Each 10-token segment is one bandit round. The context is the external-CPU bin, the arm is the strategy, and the cost is the measured seconds per token of the segment just generated (the first segment, which contains prefill, is skipped). Features:

  • Warm start from the offline DQN's Q-values with 2 pseudo-observations per arm.
  • Discounted estimates (discount 0.98 per round in a bin) so stale data fades when the machine drifts.
  • UCB-style exploration bonus (0.08) so arms not tried recently are re-checked, at the price of one segment.
  • Stall rejection (v4.1): a segment more than 2x slower than the best estimate in its bin is ignored, unless 5 arrive in a row, in which case the bin is reset and relearned.
  • Bin hysteresis: the bin only changes within a request when the reading moves more than bin_margin points past an edge (v5: edges 51% and 63%, margin 4).
  • One instance keeps learning across all requests. Its state is saved to adaptive_bandit_state_*.json so interrupted runs can resume.

4.6 Offline RL component

The DQN (src/rl/dqn_agent.py) is an MLP with two hidden layers of 64 units, input [prompt_length, input_tokens, cpu%, ram%] and one Q-value per strategy. Since requests are independent, it is trained as a contextual bandit with gamma=0. The logged data contains the outcome of every strategy in every context, so all three rewards are learned per context (full feedback). The reward is negative seconds per generated token. CPU and RAM penalties exist in src/rl/reward.py but are off by default, because post-run snapshots were too noisy. Features are built by one shared function (src/rl/features.py) for training and serving.

6. Phase 2 (v3): real strategies and a corrected methodology

Commit 5291ab2 changed the strategies so they execute differently by enforcing thread budgets {3, 6, 10} across stages (with balanced running two sequential stages at 3 threads each, still fp32 and token-identical). It also changed the measurement protocol. Strategies are now run back to back in random order within the same context (prompt, repeat, load level), after warm-up, under a controlled number of background busy workers. The state is measured before the decision, real generated token counts are used, and results come with bootstrap 95% CIs and paired differences.

7. Phase 3: contextual-bandit DQN

The DQN was trained for 200 episodes on the v3 data with full feedback, with prompts split into train and held-out validation sets. On the 15 held-out contexts it had zero regret in 6 of them and a mean regret of 0.049 s per token against the oracle (best strategy in hindsight). It learned the main pattern in the data: mostly static at 0-2 workers and balanced at 4-8 workers. The model is saved as data/processed/dqn_model_v3.pth and is the offline policy used in all later runs.

8. Phase 4: intra-generation re-scheduling (v3 adaptive)

Commit 3f42bdb added re-scheduling during generation and evaluate_adaptive.py. Four scenarios: steady_idle, steady_heavy, spike (load arrives mid-request) and relief (load leaves mid-request). Six conditions were compared on 60-token requests with time-triggered load, 3 prompts per scenario.

The legacy v3 analysis reported no clear benefit for intra-generation scheduling and a 35% descriptive slowdown versus fixed latency_optimized in spike. Its small sample and earlier unadjusted statistical procedure do not support a current significance claim. Short requests, time-triggered rather than token-triggered load, blocking telemetry, and possible system drift were proposed as explanations, not isolated causal findings.

  • The historical report observed mean differences of -7.8% in spike and -7.0% in relief versus the per-request DQN. Its significance labels were not adjusted across comparisons and have not been recomputed; treat these as descriptive only.
  • It corrected a stale offline model. The v3 data said balanced wins under load. At v4 time the 6-thread latency_optimized won everywhere. The DQN kept choosing balanced; the bandit moved to latency_optimized.

The resulting implementation added outlier rejection, bin hysteresis, slower forgetting (discount 0.98), and a smaller exploration bonus (0.08).

The sweep shows three distinct thread-budget regimes and an oversubscription cliff. Up to 6 workers, all thread budgets are within about 15% of each other. At 8 workers, 6 threads (latency_optimized) wins. At 10-14 workers, 3 threads (balanced, running 3+3 threads sequentially) wins. These regimes set the v5 bandit bin edges (51%, 63%) and the rule threshold (60%).

11.4 Reaction delay in the spike scenario

In spike, all three adaptive policies lose approximately 21.5-21.8 s to fixed balanced (37.7 s). With all contexts retained, rl_adaptive averaged 59.50 s (paired diff +21.80 s, 95% CI [7.58, 35.54], raw p = 0.020, Holm p = 0.44). rule_adaptive averaged 59.45 s (paired diff +21.75 s, raw p = 0.0057, Holm p = 0.14). The adaptive policies switch late because with a 10-token decision interval and patience 2, they require 10-15 tokens at approximately 2.5 s per token under 8 workers before leaving static.

Re-deciding DQN and the 60% threshold rule are statistically indistinguishable. The gain comes from intra-generation re-deciding, not from learning.

12. Methodology and statistics

  • Paired, randomized measurements: all conditions run back to back on the same prompt under the same load scenario, in shuffled order, after a warm-up generation. This removes the run-order confound of v1/v2.
  • Pre-decision state: [prompt_length, input_tokens, cpu%, ram%] is measured before the request, identically in training and serving.
  • Fixed-length requests: v4/v5 generate exactly 200 tokens, so latency is comparable.
  • Load change at a token index, not a time, so every policy faces it at the same point of the response.
  • Statistics: v5r reports 95% paired bootstrap CIs, paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Holm correction spans each predeclared family across all scenarios and baselines. The legacy significant field aliases the adjusted paired-t test; a separate flag reports whether the bootstrap CI excludes zero. The older v3-v5 report labels used a different unadjusted rule.
  • Exclusions: the original v5 prose applied a latency-only outlier rule. Because the source has no page-fault, paging, or swap counters, v5r excludes no contexts in its primary analysis and presents the four heuristic flags only as sensitivity candidates.
  • Output equivalence is checked on token IDs for every context.
  • Handoff timings are in-process clone() copies, a lower bound on a real inter-device transfer.

13. Engineering, testing and reproducibility

  • Tests: the focused pipeline and adaptive policy suites cover unit logic and mock pipelines. The real-model tests/test_functional_equivalence.py runs the full TinyLlama-1.1B engine and verifies token-level equivalence against monolithic generate().
  • evaluate_adaptive.py supports --resume, --report-only, configurable scenarios, decision intervals 5/10/20, patience 0/1/2, thread budgets 2/4/6/8/10/12, a decision-boundary emergency threshold, and --keep-awake. The new cores_heuristic and rule_noh policies are covered by deterministic tests. segment_oracle is an offline-only utility and was not scored on v5 because per-segment counterfactual outcomes are absent.
  • run_overnight.cmd resumes the v5 run unattended with HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 and appends to a log.
  • Every data version is written under its own tag (v2, pilot, v3, v4, v5). Nothing older is overwritten.
  • Environment quirks: the report metadata describes a 10-physical-core, 16-logical-core, 16 GB laptop and CPU-only float32 TinyLlama. The v5 raw run CSV does not record page-fault or swap activity; high RAM snapshots and latency spikes alone do not prove paging. Analysis environment versions and collection-time metadata gaps are documented in the v5r text report.

14. Limitations and open problems

  1. Small samples: v5r has 8 contexts per scenario. Several paired intervals remain wide, and selecting the best fixed mean from the same observations is descriptive rather than an independent confirmatory comparison.
  2. One machine, one model, CPU only. The thread-budget regimes are specific to this 10-core / 16-thread laptop and will move on other hardware.
  3. Paging: the raw v5 table lacks independent paging telemetry. Four latency-only candidates are marked in the v5r audit, not treated as verified paging events; an earlier manually removed context is not present in the supplied source CSV, so its alleged corruption cannot be independently evaluated here.
  4. Synthetic load: busy-loop worker processes are a simple model of contention. Real co-located workloads also compete for memory bandwidth and cache.
  5. Reaction delay: the all-context v5 mean in spike is descriptively higher for adaptive policies than fixed balanced; adjusted tests do not establish the cause or a general effect.
  6. Oracle evaluation requires matched per-segment counterfactual outcomes that are not present in the current data.
  7. The offline DQN goes stale when machine conditions change (seen between v3 and v4). It was trained on loads up to 8 workers only.
  8. The "partitioning" is within one device. Layer stages run sequentially, so stage splits only change thread budgets, not parallelism. A CPU/GPU split (RTX 3050) has not been tried.
  9. The execution graph in src/graph is descriptive (5 coarse nodes plus partition metadata). The scheduler does not search over graph partitions.

15.3 DQN v6 training and offline validation

Contextual-bandit DQN training (gamma = 0) was executed on data/processed/training_data_v6.csv (144 rows, 24 paired contexts). Prompts 21-40 remained strictly held out.

  • Training trajectory (100 episodes): Average loss converged from 0.7343 to 0.0562 (92.3% decrease), and cumulative reward improved from -12.859 to -10.420. Model weights were saved to data/processed/dqn_model_v6.pth.
  • Offline evaluation on held-out contexts (repeat 2, 8 contexts): Mean regret was 0.0112. The RL policy achieved a mean latency of 13.909 s versus the theoretical per-context oracle of 13.629 s (within 2.0% of the upper bound). By comparison, fixed 10 threads averaged 64.979 s, fixed 6 threads averaged 63.462 s, and fixed 2 threads averaged 14.280 s.
  • Scope and limitation: The training and validation sets were formed across background contention conditions for Prompt 1 (repeats 0 & 1 for training, repeat 2 for validation). Consequently, generalization to unseen prompts has not yet been demonstrated in this offline experiment.

16. Phase 8 (v7): pre-registered main run

v7 replaced the three-strategy action space with single-stage thread budgets (2, 3, 4, 6, 10 threads, plus balanced = 3+3 sequential stages). It added per-10-token segment logging with a machine-wide paging counter (Windows "Pages Input") and a RAM guard that waits, then skips a run if RAM is above 85% before it starts. All policies were built and frozen before the main run:

  • rule_lookup maps measured external CPU to the best arm from the v7 load sweep (bins at 63.8 / 75.1 / 83.8% to threads_6 / threads_4 / balanced / threads_2). Decision interval 10 and patience 1 were chosen in a reaction-delay pilot (experiments/e2_frozen.yaml).
  • bandit_adaptive is the online bandit, warm-started with a prior built from the same sweep (experiments/e4_bandit_prior.yaml).
  • rl_adaptive is a DQN retrained on the sweep. In leave-one-prompt-out evaluation it picked the best arm in 35% of held-out contexts (56 / 31 / 19% per fold) with mean regret 0.0053 s/token, against 79% and 0.0014 s/token for the fold-built lookup rule (experiments/e3_dqn_v7.txt). It stayed in the plan as a pre-registered comparison.
  • cores_heuristic picks a thread count from the number of idle cores.

16.1 Design (experiments/e4_plan.yaml)

  • 10 prompts (IDs 21-30) x 5 scenarios = 50 contexts, 9 conditions, 450 runs of 200 tokens. Condition order is randomised within each context with seed 11.
  • Fixed arms: threads_2, balanced (3+3), latency_optimized (6), static (10). Adaptive: rule_v5 (old 60% threshold), rule_lookup, cores_heuristic, rl_adaptive, bandit_adaptive.
  • Primary analysis, as pre-registered: 20 paired t-tests on per-context latency (rule_lookup vs balanced and vs threads_2; rl_adaptive vs rule_lookup; cores_heuristic vs rule_lookup; each in 5 scenarios), Holm correction over all 20 at alpha 0.05. Wilcoxon signed-rank p and 95% bootstrap CIs are reported alongside, not as the decision test. With n = 10 the smallest possible two-sided Wilcoxon p is 2/1024 = 0.002, so a Wilcoxon p of 0.002 means all 10 pairs point the same way. No exclusions. Sensitivity: the same tables with paging-flagged runs removed.
  • Everything else, including all comparisons with bandit_adaptive, latency_optimized, static, rule_v5 and the best fixed arm, is descriptive.
  • Amendment (18 Oct, before any analysis of main-run outcomes): prompts 21-30 instead of 21-35 (675 to 450 runs) because of limited machine time. Conditions, scenarios, seeds and the test family were unchanged, and the 67 runs already made matched the amended order. The resume plan had printed median latencies per condition from those 67 runs, as a time estimate, before the amendment.

16.7 Reading

  • Policies built on direct measurements of this machine beat the offline DQN. rule_lookup (a lookup table from the load sweep) and bandit_adaptive (an online bandit warm-started from the same sweep) are both close to the per-segment oracle. rule_lookup is within 0.15 s of it in four scenarios and bandit_adaptive within 1.25 s; both are within 1.41 s in ramp. In mean latency they are within 1.2 s (5.5%) of each other in every scenario, with bandit_adaptive slightly ahead in ramp (21.96 vs 22.15 s) and rule_lookup ahead elsewhere. The bandit result is descriptive, since it was not in the test family.
  • The DQN did not generalise across prompts. It picked the best arm in 35% of held-out contexts offline, and online it was 10% to 60% slower than rule_lookup in four of five scenarios. It also switched far more (10.3 switches per request in steady_heavy vs 2.6). This is a result about this DQN, trained on 3 sweep prompts with this state; it is not evidence that learning-based scheduling loses in general.
  • Re-deciding during generation pays off most when load changes mid-request: in spike, rule_lookup beat the best fixed arm in all 10 contexts. Under steady load the best fixed arm is almost as good, provided one knows in advance which arm that is.
  • Fixed static (10 threads) still falls off the oversubscription cliff in ramp and is the worst arm under load. On an idle machine, 6 and 10 threads are within about 2% of each other.

  • All 450 runs present, 50 of 50 contexts complete, token IDs identical across conditions in every context, every run generated 200 tokens, fixed arms never switched, no duplicate cells (experiments/check_main_final.txt).

  • Speed matches across the two sessions.   • RAM before runs: min 79.8%, median 82.7%, max 85.0%. The guard skipped 17 runs (skipped_runs_v7.csv); all 17 were run later with --resume. The run process was restarted once on 19 Oct during a guard wait; the run in progress had not been written and was redone.

16.9 Threats to validity

  • Power settings changed between sessions. On 17 Oct the laptop gave only about 37% CPU to 16 busy workers (core parking in the Balanced scheme). The AC core-parking and scheduling settings were changed and python was exempted from power throttling before the 18-19 Oct session, so the 67 runs from 13 Oct and the 383 later runs were made under different power settings. The settings in force on 13 Oct were not recorded; the 17 Oct values before the change are in experiments/powercfg_before.txt. Gates guarded each resume: CPU capacity at 100% with 16 workers, and threads_2 and balanced within 25% of the sweep. The by-session comparison (16.8) shows fixed-arm speed within about 1% for most arms, but it rests on n = 1-2 runs per arm from 13 Oct.
  • Uneven timing within contexts. Pairing assumes all conditions of a context run close together. Context 8 spans 13 and 18 Oct. Context 9 (prompt 22, relief) ran 3 conditions on 18 Oct at 23:59 and 6 on 19 Oct at 08:44. Contexts 46-48 each span up to 2 hours because of RAM-guard skips.
  • Memory pressure. With the fp32 model loaded, RAM sat at 80-86%, and 106 of 450 runs had a paging event. The sensitivity analysis keeps every direction, but at n = 3-9.
  • Plan amendment after the start. The amendment to prompts 21-30 was made after 67 runs, and after median latencies from those runs had been printed for a time estimate. It changed only the number of prompts.
  • Best-fixed comparison is chosen in hindsight from the same data. That favours the fixed arm, and it is descriptive.
  • Scope. One laptop (10 physical / 16 logical cores, 16 GB), one 1.1B model in fp32 on CPU, synthetic busy-loop load, 10 prompts. rule_lookup's arm map was measured on this machine, which is part of why it does well here; a different machine needs a new sweep.
